<WhyItMatters>
A home is the biggest purchase most people make, and "rent is throwing money away" is some of the most repeated money advice there is. It isn't that simple. Whether buying beats renting depends mostly on how long you stay, the price compared with rent, and what you'd do with the money otherwise. With a master's, a job and then a PhD likely in different places over the next several years, this lesson is mostly about knowing when the time is right.
</WhyItMatters>

## How much house can you afford?

Lenders look at your <Term id="dti">debt-to-income ratio</Term>: your monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income. Different lenders set different limits <Cite id="cfpb-dti" />. A common rule of thumb (not a legal limit) is to keep housing costs under about 28% of gross income and all debt payments under about 36%. What a lender will approve is the maximum, not a recommendation.

The true monthly cost of owning is much more than the mortgage payment:

- **Principal and interest** on the mortgage.
- **Property tax.** In California, the base rate is 1% of the assessed value (usually the purchase price), plus local additions <Cite id="ca-boe-property-tax" />.
- **Homeowners insurance.**
- **<Term id="pmi">PMI</Term>** if your down payment is under 20% on a conventional loan <Cite id="cfpb-pmi" />.
- **Maintenance and repairs.** A common rule of thumb is to budget about 1% of the home's value per year.
- **HOA fees**, for many condos and townhouses.

On top of that are **closing costs** when you buy and **selling costs** (mostly agent fees) when you sell. Closing costs typically run 2% to 5% of the purchase price <Cite id="cfpb-how-much-to-spend" />, and selling costs add several percent more. That's why buying rarely pays off if you'll move in a few years.

<Example title="Worked example: a $450,000 condo vs. $2,200 rent">
Using the calculator's default assumptions: 10% down, a 6.5% mortgage, a 3% yearly rise in home prices and rent, and a 5% return on money invested instead.

- **Cash needed to buy:** $58,500 (down payment plus closing costs).
- **Mortgage payment:** about $2,560 a month, before taxes, insurance and maintenance. That's already more than the rent.
- **After 7 years**, the renter who invested the down payment and the monthly difference comes out about **$42,000 ahead** in this example. With these assumptions buying doesn't catch up within 7 years.

Change one assumption, such as faster price growth, a lower rate, or a longer stay, and the answer can flip. That's the point of running your own numbers.
</Example>

<ExpertsDisagree>
**"Owning builds wealth."** A long-run study of 16 countries from 1870 to 2015 found that housing, counting both price gains and the rent you'd otherwise pay, earned returns about as high as stocks, with smaller swings <Cite id="jorda-et-al-2019" />. A mortgage also acts as forced savings for people who wouldn't otherwise save.

**"Renting and investing can do just as well."** Those housing returns are averages across whole countries. A single home is far less diversified than an index fund, buying and selling costs are high, and a renter who reliably invests the difference can match or beat a buyer, especially over shorter periods.

Both are true in different situations. The calculator shows which way your own numbers point.
</ExpertsDisagree>

<KeyTakeaways>
- The cost of owning is much more than the mortgage: taxes, insurance, maintenance, PMI and HOA fees.
- Buying and selling costs mean buying usually only wins if you stay several years.
- Compare against renting **and investing the difference**, not against renting alone.
- What a lender approves is a ceiling, not a target.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Comparing rent with the mortgage payment alone.**
- **Buying when you expect to move in a few years.**
- **Spending your emergency fund on the down payment**, leaving nothing for the first repair.
- **Assuming home prices always rise.** They can fall for years, as they did nationally after 2006, when they fell about a third by late 2011 <Cite id="sp-case-shiller-2012" /> <Cite id="fred-case-shiller" />.
</CommonMistakes>
