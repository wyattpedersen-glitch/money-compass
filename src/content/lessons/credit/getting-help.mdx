<WhyItMatters>
Debt problems usually get worse quietly, and the earlier you act, the more options you have. This lesson is about recognizing the warning signs, knowing who can actually help (and who just wants your money), and understanding the last-resort options so they're less scary if you or someone you know ever needs them.
</WhyItMatters>

## Warning signs

The FTC's advice on getting out of debt starts with being honest about where you are: list every debt, and build a realistic budget before anything else <Cite id="ftc-debt-relief" />. Signs that debt is becoming a problem include only managing minimum payments, using credit for basic needs, borrowing from one place to pay another, and missing payments.

## Who can help

- **Your lenders.** Call before you fall behind. Many will offer a payment plan or hardship program.
- **A nonprofit credit counselor.** Reputable counselors review your whole budget, usually for free or a small fee. They can set up a **debt management plan**, where you make one monthly payment to the agency, which pays your creditors, often at lower interest rates <Cite id="nfcc" /> <Cite id="ftc-debt-relief" />.
- **A HUD-approved housing counselor** if you're behind on rent or a mortgage, or facing eviction or foreclosure. Note that HUD approves *housing* counselors. For credit card and other debt, look for a nonprofit credit counselor instead <Cite id="hud-housing-counselors" />.

## Who to be careful with

**Debt settlement companies** promise to negotiate your debts down for a fee. The FTC warns that they often tell you to stop paying your creditors, which damages your credit and adds late fees, with no guarantee creditors will agree. It's illegal for companies selling debt relief by phone to charge fees before they settle a debt. And forgiven debt may be taxable income <Cite id="ftc-debt-relief" />.

**Debt collectors** must follow federal rules. They can't harass you or lie to you, and you can ask them to prove the debt is yours <Cite id="cfpb-debt-collection" />.

## Bankruptcy basics

Bankruptcy is a legal process in federal court for people who can't repay their debts <Cite id="uscourts-bankruptcy" />. The two common types for individuals:

- **Chapter 7** sells certain property to pay creditors and wipes out many remaining debts, usually within a few months. There's an income test to qualify.
- **Chapter 13** sets up a three- to five-year repayment plan, and lets you keep property like a home if you keep up with the plan.

Some debts usually survive bankruptcy, including most taxes, child support, and **student loans** unless you prove "undue hardship" in a separate court case <Cite id="uscourts-bankruptcy" />. Before filing, most people must take a credit counseling course from an agency approved by the U.S. Trustee Program <Cite id="doj-ust-counseling" />. A bankruptcy can stay on your credit report for up to ten years <Cite id="cfpb-negative-info" />.

<Example title="Worked example: choosing the right help">
Your friend has $9,000 across three credit cards at 25% to 29% APR, pays only the minimums, and just missed a payment.

1. **First**, they list the debts and stop using the cards.
2. **Then** they call each card company to ask about a hardship program.
3. **If that isn't enough**, they contact a nonprofit credit counselor. A debt management plan could lower the rates and roll everything into one payment.
4. **They avoid** a debt settlement company that wants them to stop paying their cards, and a payday loan to cover the missed payment.

Bankruptcy is for when the debt can't realistically be repaid at all. At that point, they should talk to a bankruptcy attorney. Many offer a free first consultation.
</Example>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Act early: call your lenders before you miss payments.
- Nonprofit credit counseling is usually free or low-cost and can lower interest rates through a debt management plan.
- Be wary of debt settlement companies and anyone charging fees up front.
- Bankruptcy is a legal last resort. It doesn't erase student loans in most cases.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Waiting until accounts are in collections** to ask for help.
- **Confusing a for-profit "debt relief" company with a nonprofit credit counselor.** Ask whether they're a nonprofit and what they charge.
- **Taking a payday loan or a cash advance** to make a payment.
- **Ignoring debt collectors.** Respond in writing and ask them to validate the debt.
</CommonMistakes>
