<WhyItMatters>
Interest is the price of borrowing. The same $5,000 can cost you almost nothing or thousands of dollars depending on the rate, how long you take to pay it back, and whether interest compounds. Understanding it is what keeps a small balance from quietly growing into a big one.
</WhyItMatters>

## Interest rate vs. APR

The <Term id="interest-rate">interest rate</Term> is what the lender charges on the money you borrowed. The <Term id="apr">APR</Term> adds certain fees on top, so it's the better number for comparing loans <Cite id="cfpb-apr-vs-interest" />. On credit cards, the APR and the interest rate are usually the same, because the card's fees aren't included in it <Cite id="cfpb-apr-vs-interest" />.

Savings accounts quote an <Term id="apy">APY</Term> instead, which includes compounding. Borrowing uses APR, saving uses APY. Comparing an APR with an APY isn't apples to apples.

## Simple vs. compound interest

**Simple interest** is charged only on the <Term id="principal">principal</Term>. **Compound interest** is charged on the principal plus interest that has already built up. That's great when you're earning it and expensive when you're paying it.

Credit cards compound: unpaid interest is added to your balance, and next month you pay interest on it too. Credit card rates are also high. The Federal Reserve's data shows that the average rate on card accounts that pay interest has been above 20% in recent years <Cite id="fed-g19" />.

The good news: if you pay the **full statement balance** by the due date each month, you usually pay no interest at all on purchases. That window is the <Term id="grace-period">grace period</Term> <Cite id="cfpb-grace-period" />. Carry even part of a balance and you typically lose the grace period, so interest starts on new purchases right away.

## Amortization: how loan payments work

Car loans, student loans and mortgages are usually paid off in equal monthly payments. This is called <Term id="amortization">amortization</Term>. Each payment covers that month's interest first, and the rest reduces the principal. Early on, the balance is large, so more of each payment goes to interest. Later, more goes to principal.

<Example title="Worked example: the minimum-payment trap">
You owe $5,000 on a credit card at 22% APR.

- **Paying $200 a month**, it takes **34 months** to pay off, and you pay about **$1,750** in interest.
- **Paying a typical minimum** (about 1% of the balance plus that month's interest, never less than $25) and charging nothing new, it takes about **19 years** (230 months) and costs about **$8,100** in interest. That's more than the original balance.

The minimum payment is designed to keep you current, not to get you out of debt.
</Example>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Compare loans by APR, not by the monthly payment.
- Credit card interest compounds and is usually above 20%. Paying the full statement balance avoids it.
- In an amortizing loan, early payments are mostly interest. Extra payments go straight to principal and save the most early on.
- Paying only the minimum on a card can take decades.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Shopping by monthly payment.** A longer loan has a smaller payment but costs more in total.
- **Paying the "minimum due"** instead of the statement balance.
- **Confusing APR and APY** when comparing a loan with a savings account.
- **Ignoring fees**, like origination fees on personal loans, that aren't obvious from the rate.
</CommonMistakes>
