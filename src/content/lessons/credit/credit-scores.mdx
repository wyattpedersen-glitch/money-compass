<WhyItMatters>
Your <Term id="credit-score">credit score</Term> affects the interest rate on a car loan or mortgage, whether a landlord approves your apartment application, and sometimes the deposit for utilities or a phone plan <Cite id="cfpb-credit-score" />. Moving for a master's, then a job, then a PhD means several apartment applications in a few years, so a solid score will save you hassle as well as money.
</WhyItMatters>

## Reports vs. scores

Your <Term id="credit-report">credit report</Term> is the raw record: your accounts, balances, payment history, and any collections or bankruptcies. Three national credit bureaus keep reports: Equifax, Experian and TransUnion <Cite id="ftc-free-credit-reports" />. The bureaus may have slightly different information, because not every lender reports to all three.

Your credit score is a number calculated from a report. The most widely used scores come from FICO and VantageScore, and the main versions run from 300 to 850 <Cite id="cfpb-credit-score" />. You don't have one score. You have many, depending on the model, the version and which bureau's data is used.

## Checking your report and score

You can get your credit reports from all three bureaus for free at **AnnualCreditReport.com**, the official site set up under federal law. The bureaus now let you check them online every week for free <Cite id="ftc-free-credit-reports" />. Watch out for look-alike sites that charge.

Checking your own report or score is a "soft" inquiry and doesn't lower your score <Cite id="cfpb-credit-reports-scores" />. Many banks and card companies also show you a free score each month.

## Fixing errors

If something on your report is wrong (an account you never opened, a payment marked late that wasn't), you can dispute it for free with the bureau that shows it and with the company that reported it. The bureau generally has to investigate within 30 days <Cite id="ftc-disputing-errors" />.

Accurate negative information can't be removed early, but it doesn't last forever. Most of it, like late payments and collections, stays for about seven years. Some bankruptcies stay for up to ten <Cite id="cfpb-negative-info" />.

<Example title="Worked example: reading a report">
Say you pull your three reports and find:

- A student credit card opened two years ago, always paid on time, with a $200 balance on a $1,000 limit. That's 20% utilization.
- A $45 medical bill in collections from an ER visit you thought insurance had covered.
- An address you've never lived at, on your Experian report only.

**What to do:** Call the hospital and your insurer about the bill first, since it may have been an insurance mistake you can fix at the source. Dispute the wrong address with Experian. And pay the card before the statement closes to bring the reported balance down.
</Example>

<Uncertain>
FICO's weights are averages for the general population. For someone with a short history, like a college student, the importance of each factor can differ <Cite id="myfico-score-factors" />.
</Uncertain>

<KeyTakeaways>
- A credit report is the record; a credit score is a number calculated from it. You have many scores, not one.
- Paying on time (35%) and keeping card balances low (30%) matter most.
- Get your reports free at AnnualCreditReport.com. Checking your own credit never hurts it.
- Dispute errors directly with the bureau. It's free.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Paying a "credit repair" company to fix accurate information.** No one can legally remove accurate, current negative items, and you can dispute real errors yourself for free <Cite id="ftc-disputing-errors" />.
- **Never looking at your report** until a landlord or lender turns you down.
- **Assuming a good income means a good score.** Income isn't part of the score.
- **Using look-alike sites** instead of AnnualCreditReport.com.
</CommonMistakes>
