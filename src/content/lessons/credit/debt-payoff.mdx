<WhyItMatters>
If you ever carry more than one debt, you'll have to decide where each extra dollar goes. The two best-known methods give different answers. One saves the most money; the other may make you more likely to finish. Knowing the trade-off lets you pick on purpose.
</WhyItMatters>

## The two methods

Both start the same way: **pay the minimum on every debt**, so nothing goes late. Then put every extra dollar on one debt at a time.

- **Avalanche:** extra money goes to the debt with the **highest interest rate**. Mathematically, this always costs the least interest.
- **Snowball:** extra money goes to the debt with the **smallest balance**. You pay off whole debts sooner, which can feel motivating.

When a debt is gone, its old minimum payment rolls into the next one, so your total payment stays the same and the "snowball" grows.

## What the research says

<ExpertsDisagree>
The math clearly favors the avalanche. The psychology is less clear.

- In lab experiments, Amar and colleagues found that people tend to pay off small debts first even when it costs them more. They called this "debt account aversion" and saw it as a mistake <Cite id="amar-et-al-2011" />.
- Gal and McShane studied real people in a debt settlement program and found that those who closed out more individual debts early were more likely to eventually eliminate all of their debt. That suggests small wins may help people stick with it <Cite id="gal-mcshane-2012" />.
- Kettle and colleagues found that **concentrating** payments on one account at a time, rather than spreading extra money across all of them, was linked to more motivation and more progress <Cite id="kettle-et-al-2016" />. Both methods do this.

The reasonable reading: avalanche saves the most money, snowball may be easier to stick with, and either beats spreading extra payments thinly. The difference in cost is often small. Try your own numbers below.
</ExpertsDisagree>

<Example title="Worked example: three debts, $500 a month">
| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Credit card | $3,000 | 24% | $90 |
| Car loan | $8,000 | 7% | $200 |
| Store card | $600 | 18% | $25 |

With $500 a month in total:

- **Avalanche** attacks the credit card first. Debt-free in **26 months** with about **$1,304** in interest.
- **Snowball** attacks the store card first and clears it in **month 3**, versus month 14 with avalanche. Debt-free in **26 months** with about **$1,338** in interest.

Here the snowball costs only about $33 more. With larger high-rate balances the gap grows.
</Example>

## Other tools

- **Call your card issuer** and ask for a lower rate. It doesn't always work, but it costs nothing to ask.
- **A debt management plan** through a nonprofit credit counselor can lower card interest rates and combine payments into one <Cite id="nfcc" />.
- **Stop adding new charges** while you pay down cards. Otherwise the balance never falls.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Always pay every minimum first. Then focus extra money on one debt at a time.
- Avalanche (highest rate first) costs the least. Snowball (smallest balance first) gives quicker wins.
- Research suggests concentrating payments matters more than which method you choose.
- If minimums are already a struggle, talk to a nonprofit credit counselor.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Spreading extra money evenly across every debt**, so nothing gets paid off quickly.
- **Skipping a minimum payment** on one debt to pay more on another. Late fees and credit damage cost more.
- **Paying down cards while still charging new purchases on them.**
- **Draining your entire emergency fund** to pay debt, then borrowing again when something breaks.
</CommonMistakes>
