<WhyItMatters>
  Most budgets fail not because the plan was wrong but because sticking to it takes willpower every single month.
  Automation flips that around: you make the good decision once, and it keeps happening unless you actively stop it. As

  easier to keep going through each change than one that depends on remembering.

## The power of the default

A "default" is what happens if you do nothing. Research keeps finding that defaults have an enormous effect on what people end up doing, even when switching is easy.

The clearest example is organ donation. In European countries where people had to opt in to be donors, consent rates were below 30%. In similar countries where people were donors unless they opted out, rates were above 85% <Cite id="johnson-goldstein-2003" />. The choice was the same. Only the default changed.

Money works the same way. Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea studied a large U.S. company that switched its <Term id="401k">401(k)</Term> from "sign up if you want to" to "you're enrolled unless you opt out." Participation among new employees jumped from **37% to 86%** <Cite id="madrian-shea-2001" />.

## Save More Tomorrow

Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi designed a program around how people actually behave. Instead of asking employees to save more now, it asked them to commit to saving more out of _future_ raises. Each time their pay went up, their savings rate went up automatically, so take-home pay never fell. In the first company to try it, participants' average saving rate rose from **3.5% to 13.6%** over 40 months <Cite id="thaler-benartzi-2004" />.

The lesson for you: when your income rises (from a stipend to a salary, or with a raise), decide ahead of time how much of the increase goes to savings, and set it up before the first bigger paycheck arrives.

## Pay yourself first, automatically

"Pay yourself first" means savings come out before spending, not from whatever is left at the end of the month <Cite id="clason-1926" />. Automation is what makes it work. The CFPB suggests setting up automatic transfers into savings, or splitting your direct deposit so part of each paycheck goes straight to a savings account <Cite id="cfpb-emergency-fund" />.

A simple setup:

1. **Payday:** your pay lands in checking.
2. **The next day:** automatic transfers move each goal's monthly amount to a separate savings account.
3. **Bills:** fixed costs (rent, phone, insurance) go out on autopay.
4. **Everything left** in checking is yours to spend without guilt.

<Example title="Worked example: automating a grad-school budget">
Say your take-home pay is $2,600 a month. Your plan sets aside $150 for an emergency fund and $50 for a Roth IRA.

- On the 2nd of each month (the day after payday), $150 moves to a high-yield savings account and $50 moves to the IRA.
- Rent ($1,400) and your phone ($40) are on autopay.
- That leaves $960 in checking for groceries, transit and fun. If it's still there at the end of the month, it can stay as a buffer.

After a year, without making a single decision, you've moved $1,800 into your emergency fund and $600 into retirement.

</Example>

<ExpertsDisagree>
  Defaults are powerful, but they don't pick the *right* amount for you. In the Madrian and Shea study, many
  automatically enrolled workers stayed at the company's low default contribution rate and its conservative default
  fund, while employees who had signed up on their own more often chose higher rates <Cite id="madrian-shea-2001" />.
  Automation keeps a plan running. It doesn't replace checking, once or twice a year, that the plan is still the right

</ExpertsDisagree>

<KeyTakeaways>
  - What happens when you do nothing is a powerful force. Make your default the good choice. - Automatic enrollment more
  than doubled 401(k) participation in one landmark study. - Committing future raises to savings worked far better than
  asking people to cut spending now. - Set transfers for the day after payday, and put fixed bills on autopay.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
  - **Scheduling transfers before your pay arrives**, which can overdraw your account. Leave a small buffer in checking.
  - **Setting it and never looking again.** Review the amounts whenever your income changes. - **Letting "lifestyle
  creep" eat every raise.** Decide what share of a raise goes to savings before it shows up. - **Keeping savings in the
  same account as spending money**, where it's too easy to spend.
</CommonMistakes>
