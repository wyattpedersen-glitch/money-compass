## Start with spending, not a magic number

The question isn't "how many millions?" It's **"how much will I spend each year, and how much of that will my savings need to cover?"**

1. Estimate yearly spending in retirement, in today's dollars.
2. Subtract income you'll get anyway, like Social Security or a pension.
3. The rest has to come from savings.

## Withdrawal rates and the "4% rule"

A withdrawal rate is the share of your savings you take out in your first year of retirement. After that, you raise the dollar amount with inflation each year.

In 1994, financial planner William Bengen tested this against U.S. market history. Starting at about **4%** and adjusting for inflation, a portfolio of 50% to 75% stocks lasted at least 30 years in every historical period he examined, including the Great Depression <Cite id="bengen-1994" />. A 1998 study by three Trinity University professors (the "Trinity study") reached similar conclusions across many stock/bond mixes <Cite id="cooley-hubbard-walz-1998" />.

That leads to a handy shortcut. **At a 4% withdrawal rate, you need about 25 times the yearly amount your savings must cover** (1 ÷ 0.04 = 25).

<Example>
You want to spend $60,000 a year in retirement (in today's dollars) and expect $20,000 a year from Social Security.

- Savings need to cover $60,000 − $20,000 = **$40,000 a year.**
- At 4%: $40,000 ÷ 0.04 = **$1,000,000** in today's dollars.
- At a more cautious 3.5%: $40,000 ÷ 0.035 ≈ **$1,143,000**.
  </Example>

<ExpertsDisagree>
The 4% rule is a starting point, not a guarantee, and experts debate it.

- **"It's too high today."** When bond yields were very low, researchers argued 4% was riskier than history suggested <Cite id="finke-pfau-blanchett-2013" />. Morningstar publishes a yearly estimate of a "safe" starting rate based on current conditions. In recent years its estimates have ranged from roughly 3.3% to 4% <Cite id="morningstar-retirement-income" />.
- **"It's too rigid."** Real retirees can adjust. "Guardrail" strategies let you start higher if you're willing to cut spending a bit after bad years and raise it after good ones <Cite id="guyton-klinger-2006" />.
- **"It's built for 30 years."** Someone retiring early, with a 40- or 50-year retirement, may want a lower rate.

What the evidence shows: around 4% has historically been reasonable for a 30-year retirement with a stock-heavy mix. A lower rate adds safety, and flexibility in spending helps as much as the starting number.

</ExpertsDisagree>

## Sequence risk: when bad years hit matters

A market crash right after you retire hurts much more than the same crash 20 years later, because you're selling investments while prices are low. This "sequence of returns" risk is why withdrawal-rate research uses historical periods instead of average returns, and why many retirees keep a few years of spending in bonds or cash.

## Social Security basics

Social Security pays a monthly benefit based on your 35 highest-earning years <Cite id="ssa-retirement" />.

- For anyone born in 1960 or later, **full retirement age is 67**. You can start as early as 62 for a permanently reduced benefit, or wait until 70 for a larger one <Cite id="ssa-full-retirement-age" />.
- You can see your own earnings record and estimated benefits by creating a free account at ssa.gov <Cite id="ssa-my-account" />.
- The program's trustees project that its retirement trust fund will run short in the 2030s. After that, incoming payroll taxes would still pay most, but not all, scheduled benefits unless Congress acts <Cite id="ssa-trustees" />. Many planners include a reduced estimate to be safe.
- Some state and local government jobs aren't covered by Social Security and offer a pension instead <Cite id="ssa-fairness-act" />.

## What this means at 21

The calculator's default shows the power of time. Thanks to compounding, someone who starts saving modestly in their twenties can need a far smaller monthly amount than someone who starts at 40. Grad school years with low income are a fine time to start small, in a Roth IRA if you have a taxable stipend or job income (see the account types lesson).

<CommonMistakes>
  - **Planning around average returns.** Real returns arrive in a bumpy sequence, and the order matters. - **Forgetting
  inflation.** $1 million in 40 years won't buy what $1 million buys today. This calculator works in today's dollars for
  that reason. - **Counting on a pension you won't vest in.** Check how many years it takes. - **Ignoring Social
  Security entirely**, or assuming it will pay 100% of today's promised benefits.
</CommonMistakes>
