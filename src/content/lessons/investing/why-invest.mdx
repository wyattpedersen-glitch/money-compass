<WhyItMatters>
Money sitting still slowly loses value, because prices rise over time. Investing is how savings keep up with rising prices and grow. And because growth builds on itself, the years you have right now, in your twenties, are worth more than any later decade. A dollar invested at 22 has decades more to compound than a dollar invested at 40.
</WhyItMatters>

## Inflation: why a dollar shrinks

<Term id="inflation">Inflation</Term> is the general rise in prices over time. The U.S. measures it with the Consumer Price Index, which tracks what a typical basket of goods and services costs month to month <Cite id="bls-cpi" />.

The Federal Reserve aims for inflation of about 2% a year over the long run <Cite id="fed-inflation-target" />. That sounds small, but it adds up. At 3% inflation, something that costs $1,000 today costs about $1,344 in ten years. Put the other way, $1,000 ten years from now buys only what about $744 buys today.

So cash under a mattress, or in an account earning less than inflation, quietly loses buying power every year.

## The time value of money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year. Today's dollar can be invested and grow, and next year's can't start until next year. This idea, the "time value of money," is behind almost every calculation on this site.

## Compound growth

<Term id="compound-interest">Compound interest</Term> means you earn returns on your original money *and* on the returns it has already earned <Cite id="investor-gov-glossary" />. In early years the effect is small. Later, the growth on past growth starts to dominate.

<Example>
Suppose you invest $100 a month starting at age 22 and earn 6% a year. By 52 (30 years) you'd have put in $36,000, and the balance would be about **$100,452**. That's nearly two-thirds growth.

Now wait until 32 to start, and invest the same $100 a month for 20 years. You'd put in $24,000 and end with about **$46,204**.

Starting ten years earlier means only $12,000 more of your own money, but more than twice the ending balance. Those extra years let growth compound on growth.

*Assumes a steady 6% yearly return compounded monthly and no fees or taxes. Real returns vary year to year.*
</Example>

A handy shortcut is the **rule of 72**: divide 72 by the yearly return to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 6%, that's about 12 years. At 8%, about 9.

## Nominal vs. "real" returns

A "nominal" return is the raw percentage. A "real" return is what's left after inflation. If an investment earns 7% and inflation is 3%, your buying power grows by about 3.9% a year, not 7%. The exact calculation is (1.07 ÷ 1.03) − 1.

## Where savings vs. investing fit

Not all money should be invested. Money you'll need within a few years, like your <Term id="emergency-fund">emergency fund</Term> or rent for next year, belongs somewhere safe and easy to reach, such as a high-yield savings account. Investing is for money you won't need for many years. That time lets you ride out the stock market's ups and downs, which the next lessons explain.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Inflation means cash slowly loses buying power. The Fed targets about 2% a year.
- Compound growth means returns earn returns. Time is the biggest ingredient.
- Starting a decade earlier can matter more than contributing more later.
- Judge returns after inflation ("real" returns).
- Invest money you won't need for years. Keep short-term money in savings.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Waiting until you "have more money."** Small amounts invested early can outgrow larger amounts invested later.
- **Treating a savings account as a long-term investment.** It's the right place for short-term money, but over decades it usually barely keeps up with inflation.
- **Ignoring inflation when setting goals.** $1 million in 40 years buys far less than $1 million today.
- **Investing money you'll need soon.** Markets can fall right when you need to sell.
</CommonMistakes>
