<WhyItMatters>
Once people know you're interested in investing, you'll hear pitches from friends, social media, "gurus" and salespeople. Most of what's marketed hardest is either expensive, risky, or both. A few simple red flags will protect you from the worst of it.
</WhyItMatters>

## Red flag 1: High returns with "no risk"

Every legitimate investment that offers higher expected returns comes with higher risk. That's the trade-off in every lesson so far. The SEC lists "guaranteed" high returns with little or no risk as a classic sign of fraud, along with pressure to act fast and unregistered sellers <Cite id="investor-gov-red-flags" />.

Ponzi schemes pay early investors with money from new investors, and often promise steady, unusually high returns. They collapse when new money dries up <Cite id="investor-gov-ponzi" />.

## Red flag 2: Day-trading courses and "trading for a living"

Courses and apps promise that the right system will let you beat the market by trading often. The evidence is overwhelming in the other direction: in a large study of day traders, fewer than 1% could predictably profit after fees <Cite id="barber-et-al-2014" />. Ordinary investors who trade frequently also earn less than those who buy and hold <Cite id="barber-odean-2000" />.

FINRA warns that frequent intraday trading, especially with borrowed money (margin), can cost you some or all of your investment, and that it generally isn't appropriate for people with limited money or experience <Cite id="finra-day-trading" />. Notice who reliably profits: the people selling the course.

## Red flag 3: Hot stock tips

A tip from a friend, a forum or an influencer usually comes after the price has already moved. Some are deliberate "pump and dump" schemes, where promoters hype a small stock, then sell to the people they convinced <Cite id="investor-gov-pump-dump" />. Even honest tips run into the evidence from the diversification lesson: most individual stocks underperform Treasury bills over their lifetimes <Cite id="bessembinder-2018" />.

## Red flag 4: High-fee products

Some products are sold more than they're bought. Common examples:

- **Funds with sales charges ("loads")** or expense ratios well above similar index funds. Fees compound against you <Cite id="sec-fees-bulletin" />.
- **Variable annuities**, which combine investments with insurance. They can have several layers of fees and surrender charges if you need your money early. The SEC advises understanding all of the costs before buying <Cite id="sec-variable-annuities" />.

A good habit is to ask any salesperson: "How are you paid on this?" Then look them up on FINRA BrokerCheck <Cite id="finra-brokercheck" /> or the SEC's adviser database <Cite id="sec-iapd" />.

## Red flag 5: Crypto as a core holding

Crypto assets have been extremely volatile, and the SEC warns that the space has had many frauds, scams and failed platforms <Cite id="investor-gov-crypto" />. When the SEC approved exchange-traded products holding bitcoin in 2024, its chair stressed that this did not endorse bitcoin, and called it a speculative, volatile asset <Cite id="sec-bitcoin-etp-2024" />.

<ExpertsDisagree>
Supporters argue that bitcoin is a scarce digital asset that may hold value over time, and some investors keep a small slice of their portfolio in it as a speculative bet. Critics point out that, unlike stocks or bonds, it produces no earnings or interest, so its price depends entirely on what others will pay. There's no mainstream evidence-based case for crypto as the *core* of a long-term portfolio. If you do buy any, keep it to an amount you could lose entirely without changing your plans.
</ExpertsDisagree>

## A quick checklist before any investment

1. Can I explain how it makes money in one sentence?
2. What are *all* the fees, including when I sell?
3. What's the worst realistic outcome?
4. Who's selling it, how are they paid, and are they registered?
5. Would a broad, low-cost index fund do the same job more cheaply?

<KeyTakeaways>
- High returns with no risk is the classic sign of fraud.
- Almost nobody profits from day trading, except the people selling courses.
- Most stock tips arrive late, and some are scams.
- Complex, high-fee products often benefit the seller more than the buyer.
- Crypto is highly speculative. Don't make it the core of your plan.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Acting fast because of a deadline or "limited spots."** Pressure is a sales tactic.
- **Trusting a pitch because a friend made money.** You hear about the winners, not the losers.
- **Buying something you can't explain.**
- **Skipping the background check** on a broker or adviser.
</CommonMistakes>
