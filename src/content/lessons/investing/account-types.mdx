<WhyItMatters>
The account you invest *in* matters almost as much as what you invest in. The U.S. tax code offers special accounts that let money grow without yearly taxes, and sometimes come with free money from an employer. Using them in the right order can be worth tens of thousands of dollars over a career. You'll likely meet several of these soon: an IRA while in school, then a 457(b) or 403(b) if you work for a city.
</WhyItMatters>

## The idea behind tax-advantaged accounts

Normally, you pay tax on investment gains as they're realized. Retirement accounts change the timing:

- **Traditional (pre-tax)** accounts: you get a tax break now, the money grows untaxed, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as income.
- **Roth (after-tax)** accounts: no tax break now, but the money grows untaxed and qualified withdrawals in retirement are **tax-free**, including all the growth <Cite id="irs-roth-iras" />.

The trade-off is about *when* you pay tax. If your tax rate is lower now than it will be in retirement, Roth usually comes out ahead. If it's higher now, traditional does. Students and people early in their careers often have low tax rates, which is why Roth accounts are popular at that stage.

In exchange for the tax benefits, there are rules. Most withdrawals before age 59½ owe a 10% additional tax on top of regular income tax, with some exceptions <Cite id="irs-topic-558" />.

## Workplace plans: 401(k), 403(b), 457(b)

These are offered through your job. Money comes straight out of your paycheck.

- A **<Term id="401k">401(k)</Term>** is the usual plan at private companies <Cite id="irs-401k-plans" />.
- A **403(b)** is the version for public schools, universities and many nonprofits <Cite id="irs-403b" />.
- A **457(b)** is common at state and local governments, including cities. One unusual feature: withdrawals from a governmental 457(b) after you leave that job don't owe the 10% early-withdrawal tax (regular income tax still applies) <Cite id="irs-457b" />. That flexibility is useful if you plan to leave a job for a PhD.

The employee contribution limit for 401(k), 403(b) and most 457(b) plans is <Fig id="401k-elective-deferral-limit" />. The 457(b) limit is separate from the 401(k)/403(b) limit, so someone offered both a 403(b) and a 457(b) can contribute to each <Cite id="irs-457b" />.

### The employer match: a guaranteed return

Many employers add money when you contribute, for example 50 cents for every dollar you put in, up to 6% of your pay. That's an instant 50% return on those dollars, before any investment growth. No investment reliably offers that. Matching money may "vest" over a few years, meaning you keep it only if you stay long enough <Cite id="irs-401k-plans" />.

### Pensions and Social Security in public jobs

Many government employers also offer a traditional pension. Pensions typically require several years of service before you're entitled to a benefit ("vesting"), so check the rules if you expect to leave after two years. Some state and local government jobs also aren't covered by Social Security <Cite id="ssa-fairness-act" />. Your benefits paperwork will say.

## IRAs: accounts you open yourself

An **<Term id="ira">IRA</Term>** is a retirement account you open at a brokerage, separate from any job <Cite id="irs-iras" />. You can contribute up to <Fig id="ira-contribution-limit" /> or your taxable compensation for the year, whichever is less.

**Good news for grad students:** since the SECURE Act of 2019, taxable stipend and fellowship payments (not tuition waivers) count as compensation for IRA purposes, so you can contribute from them even without a regular W-2 job <Cite id="irs-pub-590a" />.

A **<Term id="roth-ira">Roth IRA</Term>** has an extra advantage: the money you contribute (not the earnings) can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty, because the IRS treats contributions as coming out first <Cite id="irs-pub-590b" />. That makes it a flexible place for long-term savings when you're young. Roth IRAs have income limits, which are unlikely to matter on a student or early-career salary <Cite id="irs-roth-iras" />.

## HSAs: the triple tax break

A health savings account is available only if you're enrolled in a qualifying high-deductible health plan. Contributions are tax-deductible, growth is untaxed, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free <Cite id="irs-pub-969" />. Unused money rolls over every year and stays yours if you change jobs. The self-only limit is <Fig id="hsa-limit-self" />.

## Regular (taxable) brokerage accounts

No special tax treatment, but no limits or withdrawal rules either. Gains on investments held longer than a year are usually taxed at lower long-term capital gains rates <Cite id="irs-topic-409" />. This is the place for money beyond the tax-advantaged limits, or for goals before retirement.

## Where should the next dollar go?

A widely used order of priorities <Cite id="bogleheads-prioritizing" />:

1. A small starter emergency fund.
2. Enough in your workplace plan to get the full employer match.
3. Pay off high-interest debt, like credit cards.
4. Finish your emergency fund (3 to 6 months of essential costs) <Cite id="cfpb-emergency-fund" />.
5. HSA, if eligible.
6. An IRA, Roth or traditional.
7. More in your workplace plan, up to the limit.
8. A taxable brokerage account.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Retirement accounts trade tax breaks for withdrawal rules. Roth means pay tax now, traditional means pay it later.
- An employer match is the best guaranteed return available. Get all of it.
- Taxable grad school stipends count as compensation for IRA contributions.
- Governmental 457(b) plans don't charge the 10% early-withdrawal tax after you leave the job.
- Follow a sensible order: emergency cushion, match, high-interest debt, emergency fund, then tax-advantaged accounts.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Not contributing enough to get the full match.** It's turning down part of your pay.
- **Opening an IRA and leaving it in cash.** Contributing is only step one. You still have to choose investments inside the account.
- **Cashing out a retirement plan when you change jobs.** You'll owe taxes and possibly the 10% penalty. Roll it over to an IRA or your new plan instead.
- **Assuming "Roth" is an investment.** It's the type of account. What's inside it is up to you.
</CommonMistakes>
