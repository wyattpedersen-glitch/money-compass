<WhyItMatters>
When you invest, you'll choose between funds that try to *beat* the market and funds that simply *are* the market. The choice sounds technical, but it's one of the most consequential money decisions you'll make. The evidence leans heavily one way, and fees are a big reason why.
</WhyItMatters>

## Two ways to run a fund

- An **actively managed fund** pays professionals to pick investments they think will do better than average. It charges for that work.
- An **<Term id="index-fund">index fund</Term>** just buys everything in a market index, like the S&P 500 (about 500 large U.S. companies) or a "total market" index (thousands of companies). Nobody is picking, so costs are very low.

Both usually come as a <Term id="mutual-fund">mutual fund</Term> or an <Term id="etf">ETF</Term>.

## The arithmetic that settles most of it

In 1991, Nobel laureate William Sharpe made a simple argument <Cite id="sharpe-1991" />. Every dollar in the market is held either by index investors or by active investors. Index investors get exactly the market's return before costs. So active investors, *as a group*, must also get exactly the market's return before costs, because together they hold everything else.

Active management costs more: higher fees and more trading. So **after costs, the average actively managed dollar must earn less than the average index dollar.** That isn't a theory about markets. It's arithmetic.

Some active managers will beat the market in any given year. The question is whether you can pick them in advance, and whether they keep it up.

## What the evidence shows

- **Most active funds trail their index, and more do over longer periods.** S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes the SPIVA scorecard twice a year comparing active funds to their benchmarks. In recent editions, the large majority of U.S. large-company funds (typically around 85% to 90% or more) trailed the S&P 500 over 15-year periods <Cite id="spiva-us" />.
- **Past winners rarely keep winning.** Studies of fund performance find little persistence among top funds once you account for their investing style and costs. Persistently bad performance, driven by high fees, is more reliable than persistently good performance <Cite id="carhart-1997" />.
- **Real skill is rare and hard to spot.** A large study concluded that few active funds earn enough extra return to cover their costs, and the ones that do are very hard to separate from the lucky ones <Cite id="fama-french-2010" />.
- **Fees predict results.** Morningstar found that a fund's expense ratio was one of the most dependable predictors of future success. The cheapest funds were more likely to survive and to outperform than the most expensive ones <Cite id="kinnel-2016" />.

<Uncertain>
SPIVA percentages vary by edition, time period and fund category. The direction of the finding has been consistent for over two decades, but check the current scorecard for exact numbers.
</Uncertain>

## Why fees matter so much

A fund's yearly fee, its <Term id="expense-ratio">expense ratio</Term>, comes out of your balance every year whether the fund does well or not. Because it compounds, a seemingly small percentage turns into a large amount of money <Cite id="sec-fees-bulletin" />. Vanguard founder John Bogle, who launched the first index fund for individual investors, built his career on this point: in investing, you get what you *don't* pay for <Cite id="bogle-2017" />.

<Example>
You invest $10,000 once and leave it for 30 years. Both funds earn 7% a year before fees.

- **Index fund, 0.05% fee:** grows at 6.95%, so $10,000 × 1.0695³⁰ ≈ **$75,063**
- **Active fund, 1% fee:** grows at 6%, so $10,000 × 1.06³⁰ ≈ **$57,435**

The 1% fee costs about **$17,600**, roughly 23% of what you'd have had. That assumes the active fund *matches* the market before fees, and most don't.
</Example>

<ExpertsDisagree>
- **"Some managers really are skilled."** True. The debate is whether investors can identify them ahead of time at a reasonable cost. The evidence above suggests that's very hard.
- **Factor investing.** Research by Eugene Fama and Kenneth French found that small companies and "value" companies (cheap relative to their accounting value) earned higher average returns historically <Cite id="fama-french-1993" />. Some low-cost funds tilt toward these "factors". Whether those premiums continue, and whether they're compensation for risk, is still debated.
- **"Index funds are too concentrated."** Index funds weight companies by size, so a few giant companies can make up a large share of the S&P 500. A total-market or global index fund spreads this out more.
</ExpertsDisagree>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Before costs, the average active dollar earns the market return. After costs, it must earn less (Sharpe).
- Most active funds trail their index over long periods, and past winners rarely persist.
- Fees are one of the best predictors of fund performance, and lower is better.
- A broad, low-cost index fund gives you the market's return minus a tiny fee, which beats most professionals over time.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Picking funds by last year's returns.** Hot streaks usually fade, and fees stay.
- **Ignoring the expense ratio.** It's listed on every fund's page. When two funds track the same index, the cheaper one is almost always the better choice.
- **Paying sales charges ("loads").** Some funds sold by brokers charge several percent just to buy in. Low-cost index funds don't.
- **Owning an "index fund" with a high fee.** A few index funds still charge 0.5% or more for the same holdings others offer for a fraction of that.
</CommonMistakes>
