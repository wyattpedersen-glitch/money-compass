<WhyItMatters>
You don't need to be an expert to invest well. A portfolio of two or three low-cost index funds, or even a single fund, can be broadly diversified, cheap and easy to maintain. Simplicity also protects you from the behavioral traps in the previous lesson: there's less to tinker with.
</WhyItMatters>

## Step 1: Choose your mix

Your asset allocation is the split between stocks, bonds and cash. It's the biggest decision you'll make, and it comes down to two questions <Cite id="investor-gov-asset-allocation" />:

- **When will you need the money?** Money for decades from now can ride out stock market drops. Money for next year can't.
- **How would you react to a big drop?** A mix you abandon in a panic is worse than a more cautious one you stick with.

People with many years until retirement usually hold mostly stocks. As retirement gets closer, they shift gradually toward bonds.

## Option A: the three-fund portfolio

A popular approach among index investors uses just three broad index funds <Cite id="bogleheads-three-fund" />:

1. **A total U.S. stock market fund**: thousands of U.S. companies of every size.
2. **A total international stock fund**: companies in developed and emerging markets outside the U.S.
3. **A total U.S. bond market fund**: a broad mix of government and high-quality corporate bonds.

That's thousands of companies and bonds in three funds, at very low cost. There's no consensus on how much of the stock portion should be international. Common suggestions range from about 20% to matching each country's share of the world market <Cite id="bogleheads-three-fund" />. The sample tool below uses 40% as an illustration.

## Option B: a single target-date fund

A target-date fund is an all-in-one fund named for a year, like "Target Retirement 2070." It holds a diversified mix and automatically shifts from stocks toward bonds as that year approaches. This shifting schedule is called its *glide path* <Cite id="investor-gov-target-date" />.

It's the simplest possible choice: one fund, no rebalancing. Two cautions from the SEC: funds with the same target year can hold quite different mixes, and fees vary, so check both before choosing <Cite id="investor-gov-target-date" />.

## Step 2: Rebalance occasionally

Over time, your mix drifts. If stocks do well, they become a bigger share than you planned, and your portfolio becomes riskier. Rebalancing means selling a bit of what's grown and buying what's lagged, to get back to your target.

Vanguard's research found no single best schedule. A reasonable approach is to check once or twice a year and rebalance only when an asset class has drifted more than about 5 percentage points from its target. The main goal is controlling risk, not boosting returns <Cite id="vanguard-rebalancing" />. The easiest way to rebalance is to direct new contributions to whatever is below its target.

<Example>
Your target is 80% stocks and 20% bonds. After a great year, your $10,000 portfolio is $8,800 in stocks and $1,700 in bonds, $10,500 total. That's 84% stocks.

To get back to 80/20, you want $8,400 in stocks and $2,100 in bonds. So you'd move $400 from stocks to bonds. Or, without selling anything, you could direct your next few contributions to bonds until you're back near 80/20.
</Example>

## Step 3: Invest regularly (dollar-cost averaging)

Dollar-cost averaging means investing a fixed amount on a schedule, like $200 every payday. If you invest from each paycheck, you're doing this naturally, and it's a great habit.

A different question is what to do with a lump sum you already have, like a gift or bonus. Historically, investing it all at once beat spreading it out over a year about **two-thirds of the time**, because markets rise more often than they fall <Cite id="vanguard-cost-averaging-2023" />. Spreading it out mainly reduces the regret of investing right before a drop. If that peace of mind keeps you invested, it can be worth the expected cost.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Your mix of stocks and bonds matters more than which specific funds you pick.
- Three broad index funds, or one target-date fund, can be a complete portfolio.
- Rebalance about once a year or when your mix drifts more than about 5 points.
- Invest automatically from each paycheck. For a lump sum, investing it all at once has usually come out ahead.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Owning a target-date fund plus other funds** without realizing the target-date fund already holds everything. It throws off the mix.
- **Picking a target date based on age alone.** Choose the year you expect to retire, and check the fund's actual mix and fee.
- **Never rebalancing**, so the portfolio quietly gets riskier than you intended.
- **Over-complicating it** with a dozen funds that overlap.
</CommonMistakes>
