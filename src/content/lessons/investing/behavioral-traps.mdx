<WhyItMatters>
The biggest threat to most people's investments isn't the market. It's their own reactions to it. Selling in a panic, chasing what's hot and trading too often are natural human instincts, and they quietly cost ordinary investors real money. Knowing the traps ahead of time makes them much easier to resist when it counts.
</WhyItMatters>

## The behavior gap

A fund's published return assumes you bought and held. Real investors move money in and out, often buying after prices rise and selling after they fall. Morningstar measures the difference between what funds earned and what their investors actually earned. Over the 10 years through 2023, it estimated that investors earned about **1.1 percentage points a year less** than the funds they owned, mostly because of poorly timed buying and selling <Cite id="morningstar-mind-the-gap-2024" />.

Another widely quoted study, DALBAR's QAIB, uses a different method and typically reports a larger gap <Cite id="dalbar-qaib" />. The exact size is debated, but the direction isn't.

## Trap 1: Trading too much

Researchers Brad Barber and Terrance Odean studied 66,465 households with discount brokerage accounts from 1991 to 1996. The 20% of households that traded most earned **11.4% a year**, while the market returned **17.9%** <Cite id="barber-odean-2000" />. Trading costs (commissions and the gap between buying and selling prices) ate the difference. Their paper's title says it all: "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth."

Day trading is worse. A study of day traders in Taiwan, one of the largest such datasets, found that fewer than 1% could predictably make money after fees <Cite id="barber-et-al-2014" />.

## Trap 2: Market timing

Market timing means trying to get out before drops and back in before rises. To win, you have to be right **twice**: when to sell and when to buy back. The market's best days often come right after its worst ones, when fear is highest, so people who sell in a panic tend to miss the rebound. Morningstar traces the behavior gap above largely to the timing of investors' purchases and sales <Cite id="morningstar-mind-the-gap-2024" />.

## Trap 3: Loss aversion and panic selling

Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that people feel losses more strongly than equal gains <Cite id="kahneman-tversky-1979" />. Their later estimates suggest a loss hurts roughly **twice as much** as an equal gain feels good <Cite id="tversky-kahneman-1992" />. That's why a 20% drop can feel unbearable even when the long-run plan hasn't changed, and why selling to "make the pain stop" is so tempting.

## Trap 4: Recency bias and chasing

People judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind <Cite id="tversky-kahneman-1974" />. After a few great years, it feels like they'll continue. After a crash, it feels like the next one is coming. Either way, recent events get too much weight. This is what drives people to pile into whatever just went up, often near the top.

## What to do when the market drops 20%

Drops of 20% or more ("bear markets") are a normal part of investing in stocks. In 2008, the S&P 500 lost 37% including dividends <Cite id="damodaran-returns" />. In early 2020, it fell by roughly a third in about a month, then regained its previous high within months <Cite id="spdji-sp500" />. Recoveries are not guaranteed to be fast. After 2008, it took several years to recover. The broad U.S. stock market has eventually recovered from every past decline, though the past is no guarantee <Cite id="damodaran-returns" />.

When it happens, here's a checklist:

1. **Don't sell in a panic.** Selling turns a temporary paper loss into a permanent real one.
2. **Check your emergency fund, not your portfolio.** If your short-term cash needs are covered, you don't have to sell anything.
3. **Keep your automatic contributions going.** You're now buying at lower prices.
4. **Rebalance if your mix has drifted far from your plan.** That means selling some bonds to buy stocks, which is the opposite of panic.
5. **In a taxable account, consider "tax-loss harvesting."** Selling at a loss and buying a similar (not identical) fund lets you deduct up to $3,000 of net losses against income a year, with the rest carried forward <Cite id="irs-topic-409" />.
6. **Look less often.** Delete the app from your home screen for a while if it helps.

<Example title="Worked example: the cost of selling at the bottom">
You have $10,000 in a stock index fund. The market drops 30%, and your balance falls to $7,000.

- **If you hold**, a 43% rebound brings you back to $10,000. That's because $7,000 × 1.43 ≈ $10,000.
- **If you sell at $7,000** and wait in cash until things "feel safe" after the market has already rebounded 30%, you buy back in having missed most of the recovery. Your $7,000 never got the 30% gain, and you're about $2,100 behind someone who held, before counting what happens next.
</Example>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Investors' own buying and selling costs them, on average, around a percentage point a year (Morningstar).
- Frequent trading has been shown to reduce returns. Almost no day traders profit.
- Losses feel about twice as bad as gains feel good, which is why panic selling is tempting.
- Big drops are normal. Having a plan for them, and an emergency fund, is what keeps you invested.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Selling after a crash "until things calm down."** By the time they do, prices have usually recovered.
- **Buying whatever went up most last year.**
- **Checking your balance daily.** More looking leads to more reacting.
- **Taking investing advice from social media**, which tends to amplify recency bias and hype.
</CommonMistakes>
