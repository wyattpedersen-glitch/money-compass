<WhyItMatters>
"Investing" isn't one thing. Cash, bonds, stocks and real estate behave very differently: some are steady but grow slowly, others grow faster but can drop sharply. Knowing what each one does lets you match your money to its job, like keeping next year's rent safe and letting retirement money grow.
</WhyItMatters>

## The big four

### Cash and cash equivalents

This means savings accounts, money market funds, certificates of deposit (CDs) and short-term Treasury bills. They're the safest option: the value barely moves. Bank deposits are insured by the FDIC up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, for each type of account ownership <Cite id="fdic-deposit-insurance" />.

**Its job:** your emergency fund and money you'll need within a few years. **The catch:** after inflation, cash has historically earned close to nothing <Cite id="damodaran-returns" />.

### Bonds

A <Term id="bond">bond</Term> is a loan to a government or company that pays you interest. Bonds are steadier than stocks, and high-quality government bonds have often held up or risen when stocks fell.

**Their job:** cushioning a portfolio and providing income. **The catch:** when interest rates rise, the prices of existing bonds fall. Longer-term bonds fall more <Cite id="sec-interest-rate-risk" />. Inflation also eats into their fixed payments. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) and I bonds are designed to adjust for inflation <Cite id="treasurydirect-tips" />.

### Stocks

A <Term id="stock">stock</Term> is a share of ownership in a company. Over long periods stocks have earned the highest returns of the major asset classes, but with the largest and most frequent drops.

**Their job:** long-term growth that beats inflation. **The catch:** they can lose a third or more of their value in a bad year, and sometimes take years to recover.

### Real estate

You can own real estate directly (a home or rental) or through real estate investment trusts (REITs), which trade like stocks. A home is both a place to live and an investment, which makes its "return" hard to compare. The rent vs. buy lesson in the credit section goes deeper.

## What history shows

These are long-run U.S. averages from Professor Aswath Damodaran's widely used public dataset, which starts in 1928 <Cite id="damodaran-returns" />. They are rounded and approximate.

| Asset | Average yearly return (approx.) | How bumpy (yearly volatility, approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| U.S. stocks (S&P 500, with dividends) | about 10% compounded | high, about 20% |
| 10-year U.S. Treasury bonds | about 4.5% | moderate, about 8% |
| 3-month Treasury bills (cash-like) | about 3.3% | low, about 3% |
| Inflation | about 3% | — |

<Uncertain>
These figures are rounded and approximate, and they shift slightly each year as new data is added. The exact current values are on [Damodaran's page](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/histretSP.html). Volatility here means the standard deviation of yearly returns: in roughly two years out of three, the return lands within that distance of the average.
</Uncertain>

Two things stand out. First, after subtracting about 3% inflation, cash has barely grown, bonds have grown modestly, and stocks have grown substantially. Second, the reward came with risk: stocks had many losing years along the way.

<Example title="Worked example: what the gap means over a career">
Using round real (after-inflation) returns of 7% for stocks, 1.5% for bonds and 0.3% for cash, $10,000 left alone for 40 years would grow to roughly:

- **Stocks:** $10,000 × 1.07⁴⁰ ≈ **$150,000**
- **Bonds:** $10,000 × 1.015⁴⁰ ≈ **$18,100**
- **Cash:** $10,000 × 1.003⁴⁰ ≈ **$11,300**

All in today's dollars. The future is not guaranteed to match the past. This only shows why long-term money usually goes mostly into stocks.
</Example>

## Putting them together

Because these assets don't move in lockstep, mixing them smooths the ride. That's diversification across asset classes (see the previous lesson). The split between them, called your asset allocation, is the biggest choice you make as an investor. It depends mainly on **when you'll need the money** and **how you'd react to a big drop**. The "simple portfolio" lesson turns this into a plan.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Cash is for safety and short-term needs. Bonds are for stability. Stocks are for long-term growth.
- Historically, stocks have earned far more than bonds or cash, but with much larger swings.
- After inflation, cash has earned close to nothing over the long run.
- Rising interest rates push existing bond prices down.
- The mix you choose matters more than the specific funds.
</KeyTakeaways>

<CommonMistakes>
- **Keeping long-term retirement money in cash** because it feels safe. Inflation is a slow but real risk.
- **Putting short-term money in stocks**, like a down payment you need in two years.
- **Assuming bonds can't lose money.** They can, especially long-term bonds when rates rise.
- **Expecting averages every year.** The 10% average for stocks includes years like −37% (2008) and +26% (2009) <Cite id="damodaran-returns" />.
</CommonMistakes>
